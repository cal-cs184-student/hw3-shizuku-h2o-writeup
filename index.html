Overview

In this assignment I built a physically-based path tracer around a BVH-accelerated scene. I implemented camera ray generation, ray-triangle and ray-sphere intersection, bounding-volume-hierarchy construction and traversal, diffuse and emissive BSDFs, direct lighting with both uniform-hemisphere and light importance sampling, multi-bounce global illumination with Russian roulette, and adaptive sampling. The through-line is the rendering equation: every lighting estimate is a Monte Carlo integral where choosing a good sampling density (cos-weighted BSDF samples, light samples, or adaptive pixel samples) is what separates a noisy image from a converged one.

Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

Camera::generate_ray maps a normalized image coordinate (x, y) onto the camera's sensor plane at z = -1: the horizontal span is [-tan(hFov/2), tan(hFov/2)] and similarly for vFov, so (0,0) and (1,1) land on the bottom-left and top-right corners. The resulting camera-space direction is normalized and rotated by the camera-to-world matrix c2w, and the ray is clipped to [nClip, fClip].

Triangle intersection uses the Möller–Trumbore algorithm. With two triangle edges e1 = p2 - p1 and e2 = p3 - p1, I solve for the barycentric coordinates (u, v) and ray parameter t in one determinant computation. A ray is rejected if u < 0, v < 0, u + v > 1, or t is outside [min_t, max_t]; otherwise max_t is tightened to t so later intersections are quickly rejected. The surface normal is the barycentric interpolation of the three vertex normals: (1 - u - v) n1 + u n2 + v n3. Sphere intersection solves the quadratic |o + t d - c|^2 = r^2 and picks the nearest valid root.

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

I build the BVH recursively. Each node owns the union bounding box of its primitives. If a node contains at most max_leaf_size primitives it becomes a leaf (storing start and end iterators into the primitive array). Otherwise I split along the axis of the box's largest extent and use std::nth_element to partition the primitives by the median of their bounding-box centroids, then recurse on the two halves. The median split always produces two non-empty halves, so the recursion is guaranteed to terminate. Box traversal uses the slab method, and intersect tests both children while keeping the ray's max_t so only the nearest primitive survives.

Timing comparison (normal shading, 16 threads): cow.dae took 0.0533s with the BVH versus 23.14s with naive O(n) traversal (~434× speedup); maxplanck.dae took 0.0365s versus 88.06s (~2412×). The larger the triangle count, the more the BVH wins, because it turns the per-ray primitive test from linear in scene size into roughly logarithmic.

Part 3: Direct Illumination

DiffuseBSDF::f returns reflectance / PI, the Lambertian BRDF. estimate_direct_lighting_hemisphere samples incoming directions uniformly on the upper hemisphere around the surface normal, casts a shadow ray for each sample, and accumulates emission * f * cos(theta) * 2*PI, dividing by the sample count. estimate_direct_lighting_importance instead samples each light source directly (sample_L), converts the returned world-space direction to object space, skips lights behind the surface, and casts one shadow ray per sample with max_t = distToLight - EPS_F; it accumulates L_i * f * cos(theta) / pdf. Delta lights are sampled once, area lights ns_area_light times. one_bounce_radiance dispatches between the two based on direct_hemisphere_sample.

Uniform hemisphere sampling wastes most of its samples on directions that never hit a light, so it is far noisier and also lacks the light's directional behavior. Importance sampling spends every sample on an actual light, so the same sample budget produces much cleaner soft shadows; higher -l values smooth the penumbra by averaging more points across the area light.

Part 4: Global Illumination

DiffuseBSDF::sample_f draws a cos-weighted incoming direction and returns both that direction and its pdf. at_least_one_bounce_radiance computes the one-bounce direct term, then samples a direction according to the surface BSDF, traces a ray in that direction, and recursively evaluates the radiance arriving at the next hit; the recursive contribution is weighted by f * cos(theta) / pdf and by the inverse continuation probability for Russian roulette (cpdf = 0.7, with the first indirect bounce always traced). Accumulated mode sums every bounce; unaccumulated mode returns only the deepest requested bounce.

Part 5: Adaptive Sampling

Adaptive sampling stops tracing a pixel once its estimate has converged. For each pixel I keep the sums s1 = Σ x_k and s2 = Σ x_k^2 of the per-sample illuminance, from which I compute the mean mu = s1 / n and variance sigma^2 = (s2 - s1^2/n) / (n - 1). Every samplesPerBatch samples I check whether 1.96 * sigma / sqrt(n) ≤ maxTolerance * mu (the 95% confidence interval relative to the mean) and, if so, stop. I record the actual sample count in sampleCountBuffer so the rate image can be generated. Below are two scenes rendered with 2048 max samples, -a 64 0.05, 1 light sample, and max ray depth 5.

The rate images show red where the pixel needed most of its sample budget (noisy regions such as soft shadows and indirect-only areas) and blue/green where it converged quickly (flat, directly-lit walls). This spends the render budget where it actually reduces noise.
